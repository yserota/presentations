Slide 1 Title
From AI to Everyday Practice: Operationalizing AI in Technical Writing
⏱ ~1 min
Good [morning/afternoon], everyone. I'm Yvonne Serota — I manage the Technical Writing team at Palo Alto Networks, where we cover identity security, PAM, cloud, and SaaS documentation across some of the most complex security products in the industry.

What I'm sharing today isn't a vision — it's what we actually built, how we built it, and what we learned along the way. Everything on these slides is in production today, used by my team every single day.
Slide 2 Context
The Palo Alto Networks TW Team
⏱ ~1.5 min
Let me set the scene. 20+ writers, four major repos — Monorepo, EPM, PAM-SH, and Conjur — dozens of product lines, globally distributed team. The quote there is real — from a writer on my team — and it captures the core problem: most of the job was finding context, not writing content.

Our baseline was 12 days average cycle time from ticket creation to published doc. And we had zero data to show leadership why we needed more capacity — every conversation was based on gut feel, not numbers.
Slide 3 Our Organization
Two Pillars: SOH & Writing Pods
⏱ ~1.5 min
So how did we structure ourselves to solve this? We split into two pillars. The Standards & Operations Hub owns tooling, infrastructure, and AI platform — led by Vita Gilin. Delivery Writing Pods are the execution layer.

The critical insight: you can't build the AI pipeline AND use it at the same time with the same people. SOH builds and governs. Pods consume and deliver. Separating these roles was essential — and not obvious to us at first.
Slide 4 The Problem
The Documentation Pain Points
⏱ ~1.5 min
Before we touch AI, I want to be honest about how broken the manual process was. These six pain points are real — I could give you specific stories for every one.

Discovery was the biggest time sink: scheduling SME calls, chasing Figma links, finding the right Confluence page — often days after R&D had already moved on. And 'no measurement' — we were making resourcing decisions with zero data. That has to change before anything else can.
Slide 5 The Approach
Not Copilot. Agentic.
⏱ ~1.5 min
The temptation was to give writers a ChatGPT window and call it done. That's what most teams did. The distinction that drove everything: a prompt is one-shot, lives in a chat window, dies there. A skill is versioned, team-shared, and works the same way every time.

The core insight on the right — "AI doesn't help if it doesn't understand your workflow, your tools, your data" — is the north star we kept returning to whenever we debated priorities.
Slide 6 The Platform
Why We Chose Cursor as Our AI IDE
⏱ ~2 min
We chose Cursor for three reasons: agent mode for multi-step autonomous task execution, the skill file system for team-shared versioned workflows, and MCP — the Model Context Protocol. MCP is the secret. It connects Cursor to any tool through a policy-controlled broker. One agent can read a Jira ticket, pull the HLD from Confluence, fetch Figma screens, and clone the GitHub branch in a single run.

The Flare → MkDocs migration was the infrastructure prerequisite — Markdown is agent-readable. We couldn't have built any of this on binary Flare files.
Slide 7 The Skills
The Skills Library We Built
⏱ ~2 min
Let me walk through the library. The biggest change since we last shared any of this: Ben Skelker refactored TW Feature Doc into two specialized skills — TW Doc Write for creating and updating docs from source material, and TW Doc Review for assessing existing pages in a fresh session. The split matters: a reviewer that knows why every sentence is there is the wrong reviewer.

Open PR handles the editorial pass and PR creation. DCI Pipeline is our team health measurement system. Day Manager synthesizes my email, calendar, and Jira every morning. Two in development: automated PR review and UI path validation.

The design principle across all of them: each skill knows exactly where to find context, what to do with it, how to validate the output, and when to hand off to a human. That's the difference between a skill and a prompt.
Slide 8 The Lifecycle
AI Embedded in Every Phase
⏱ ~1.5 min
The ADLC — AI-augmented Documentation Lifecycle. Key point: AI isn't bolted on at the end. It's embedded in every phase.

TW Doc Write covers Discovery + Authoring — phases III and IV. TW Doc Review is now live in phase V Validation, running in a fresh session so the reviewer has no memory of the authoring choices.

Human writers are deliberately in the loop at the judgment gates — reviewing context quality after discovery, approving the final merge. The orange "Human" blocks are there by design — not because we couldn't automate them, because those are the moments where domain expertise matters.
Slide 9 Deep Dive
Write, Refine, Review: Three Roles, Two Skills
⏱ ~2 min
Let me show you the two-skill workflow in detail. TW Doc Write: writer types one command, agent gathers all context via MCP, pins the style guide, writes the complete Markdown draft. Writer reviews and refines.

Then — and this is new — the writer opens a fresh session and invokes TW Doc Review. Fresh session is deliberate: a reviewer that knows why every sentence is there is the wrong reviewer. The review agent checks style guide conformance, technical accuracy, source coverage, and completeness.

Discovery + draft: under 30 minutes. Quality review: minutes, not a review cycle.
Slide 10 Broader Impact
AI Across the Whole TW Operation
⏱ ~1.5 min
Once you've built the infrastructure, the same agent capabilities apply to every part of how a TW team operates. I want to highlight QBR prep specifically: before every quarterly review with leadership, the agent now auto-builds a full slide deck from live Jira and dashboard data. That used to take me half a day. Now it takes one command.

The point is: tool connectivity is a platform, not a feature. Once MCP is wired up, the use cases multiply fast.
Slide 11 Content Quality
The Documentation Health Initiative
⏱ ~1.5 min
Parallel to authoring, we built a documentation health initiative. 978 topics catalogued across 10 product spaces. Every topic scored against quality criteria, then traffic-weighted with Adobe Analytics — so a RED topic with 5,000 monthly pageviews gets priority over a RED topic with 50.

The agent generates a continuously updated prioritized remediation queue. This is how you manage content quality at scale without a manual audit team.
Slide 12 Day in the Life
A Day in the Life: Before & After
⏱ ~1.5 min
I want to make this tangible. Left column: morning chasing context via emails and Slack, a 30-min SME call to learn things already written in design docs nobody shared, afternoon drafting from scratch, end of day following up on a PR open for a week.

Right column: Day Manager surfaces three priorities, agent generates draft in 30 min, afternoon reviewing and refining — not formatting, end of day one command to open the PR. The job changed from production worker to content architect.
Slide 13 Impact
The Numbers
⏱ ~1.5 min
Here's where we are in numbers. ≤3 days vs 12-day baseline — we're tracking toward that. 10 skills in the library, 8 live — that includes the new TW Doc Write and TW Doc Review that Ben Skelker shipped. DCI target range 0.80–0.95 is the sustainability zone.

The non-obvious win at bottom right: the DCI pipeline gave us the data to have a different conversation with leadership about resourcing — with a chart, not a feeling.
Slide 14 Role Evolution
From Technical Writer to Content Architect
⏱ ~2 min
This is the most important slide. The job doesn't disappear — it elevates. Look at what's crossing out: drafting from scratch, chasing context, ad-hoc unmeasured AI use. Look at what replaces it: reviewing agent drafts, making information architecture decisions, working in a fully measured workflow.

The new job description at the bottom is what I give to candidates now: "The Content Architect decides what to say, how to structure it, and whether the agent got it right. They're the person who knows the user well enough to catch what the agent misses." That's a higher-value job.
Slide 15 What We Learned
Lessons Learned
⏱ ~2 min
Six real lessons from 18 months.

Infrastructure first — the Flare migration was painful and essential.
Start with one skill and make it excellent before expanding.
Connect your tools first — without MCP, the agent is blind.
Keep the human in the loop deliberately — the feedback loop is how skills improve over time.
Measure before you optimize — we didn't know our cycle time was 12 days until we measured it.
And: your team IS the AI strategy. Tools commoditize. People who know how to use them don't.
Slide 16 For You
What You Can Take Home Today
⏱ ~2 min
Five things to take home. [Walk through each.]

Emphasize #3 most strongly: connect your tools before you build features. The single most common mistake: building sophisticated AI automations before giving the AI any data to work with. An agent that can see your Jira, Confluence, and GitHub is categorically more capable than one that can't.

MCP connectivity is the prerequisite, not the afterthought.
